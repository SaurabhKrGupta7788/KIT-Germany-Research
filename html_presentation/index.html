Block 1 · 01 — Foundations

Microscopic Foundations of Pedestrain Dynamics

Understanding the Social Force Model (SFM) — the building block of pedestrian simulation

02 / 17
What is the Social Force Model?
The SFM treats every pedestrian as a particle influenced by forces — just like a ball rolling on a surface, but with human psychology added. Each person is driven by a desired direction but is repelled by others and by walls.
The Three Forces Acting on a Pedestrian

1. Desire Force — pulls the pedestrian toward their goal at a desired speed. This is the "engine" that makes them move.

2. Repulsive Force — pushes pedestrians away from each other and from walls. Think of it as a personal bubble that people try to maintain.

3. Attractive Force — (optional) pulls pedestrians toward friends, landmarks, or exits. Often weaker than repulsion.

Mathematical Formulation
The total force on pedestrian $i$ is the sum of all these contributions:
$$m_i \frac{d\vec{v}_i}{dt} = \vec{f}_i^{\text{desire}} + \sum_{j \neq i} \vec{f}_{ij}^{\text{rep}} + \sum_{j \neq i} \vec{f}_{ij}^{\text{attract}} + \vec{f}_{i}^{\text{wall}}$$
Key Insight SFM is a powerful microscopic model, but its purely reactive nature causes unrealistic gridlock in dense counter‑flow — a problem that CAFM solves with anticipatory "radar" steering.
Block 1 · 02 — Anticipatory Dynamics

Collision Avoidance Force Model (CAFM)

Giving pedestrians a "radar: R_saftey & R_danger" — proactive steering instead of reactive pushing

03 / 17
What is CAFM?
CAFM extends the Social Force Model by adding forward‑looking anticipation. Instead of only reacting when someone is already too close, CAFM gives each pedestrian a mental "radar" that scans the path ahead.
The Anticipation Mechanism
Using R_safety metric, the pedestrian projects their velocity forward to detect potential collisions. The model calculates the Time‑to‑Collision (TTC) and minimum distance ($d_{min}$) to nearby pedestrians.
Mathematical Formulation
The avoidance force scales with the proximity and urgency of the threat:
$$\vec{f}_{\text{CAFM}} = A \exp\left(-\frac{d_{\text{min}}}{R_{\text{safety}}}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{t_{\text{coll}}} \hat{n}_{\text{avoid}}$$
$A$: force amplitude, $R_{\text{safety}}$: perceptual safety radius, $t_{\text{coll}}$: time‑to‑collision.

Research Problem

Motivation: The High-Dimensional HPC Bottleneck

Exhaustive Parameter Exploration of Agent Simulations is Computationally Intractable

04 / 17
Curse of Dimensionality in CAFM
CAFM behavior is governed by 6 sensitive continuous parameters. Mapping macroscopic observables (Crossing Time, Flow Rate, Order Parameter) across a modest $10$-point grid per dimension requires $10^6$ simulation points.

HPC Time Cost Analysis:

• 1 CAFM Monte Carlo Ensemble (10 seeds) = 124 seconds
• Full Grid Search ($10^6$ evaluation points) = 1,435 CPU Days
• Conclusion: Blind passive sampling is very expensive on modern supercomputers.

Solution Framework
Construct a continuous 6D Gaussian Process Surrogate combined with an Active Learning race to iteratively discover blind spots and transition boundaries using under $300$ simulations.

Data Architecture

Stochastic Simulation Pipeline & Noise Quantification

256 Quasi-Monte Carlo Configurations × 10 Seeds = Ensembled Ground Truth

05 / 17
Sampling Strategy
To initialize the surrogate without clustering bias, an initial $N=256$ Sobol QMC sequence was executed across the 6D hypercube.
Monte Carlo Ensembling
Due to micro-stochastic initial positions, each parameter point $x_i$ was evaluated across $10$ distinct random seeds. This yields both mean response $Y_{\text{mean}}$ and seed variance $Y_{\text{std}}^2$:
$$y_i = \frac{1}{10}\sum_{s=1}^{10} f(x_i, \text{seed}_s), \quad \sigma_{n,i}^2 = \frac{1}{9}\sum_{s=1}^{10} (f(x_i, \text{seed}_s) - y_i)^2$$

Probabilistic Engine

Heteroscedastic Gaussian Process Regression

Analytical Bayesian Posterior Mean & Epistemic Uncertainty Quantification

07 / 17
Question
How does a Gaussian Process model CAFM parameter surfaces while outputting calibrated point-wise confidence intervals?
Mathematical Formulation
Given training data $\mathbf{X}$, prediction at candidate point $x^*$ yields a Gaussian posterior $\mathcal{N}(\mu(x^*), \sigma^2(x^*))$:
$$\mu(x^*) = \mathbf{k}_*^T [\mathbf{K} + \mathbf{\alpha}]^{-1} \mathbf{y}$$ $$\sigma^2(x^*) = k(x^*, x^*) - \mathbf{k}_*^T [\mathbf{K} + \mathbf{\alpha}]^{-1} \mathbf{k}_*$$
Scientific Conclusion
The variance term $\sigma^2(x^*)$ directly measures epistemic model ignorance, providing the ideal mathematical signal to drive active learning acquisition functions.
Interactive GP Regression Fitting Prior Samples → Observations → Posterior Confidence Interval
Kernel Optimization

Matérn 5/2 Kernel & Lengthscale

How the GP measures "similarity" between points in 6D space

08 / 17
Kernel Choice
We deploy a Matérn 5/2 kernel — it assumes functions are twice differentiable, which is ideal for crowd dynamics that can have sudden phase changes (free-flow → gridlock) but without the "ringing" artifacts of an infinitely smooth RBF kernel.
$$k(x, x') = \sigma_f^2 \left(1 + \sqrt{5}d + \frac{5}{3}d^2\right) \exp(-\sqrt{5}d)$$
What is a Lengthscale?
The lengthscale $l_i$ controls how far the influence of a training point extends in dimension $i$:
$$d^2(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \sum_{i=1}^{6} \frac{(x_i-x_i')^2}{l_i^2}$$
  • • Short $l_i$ → function changes rapidly → high sensitivity
  • • Long $l_i$ → function changes smoothly → low sensitivity
Next Step
By learning a separate lengthscale per dimension (ARD), the GP automatically discovers which parameters are most influential — we'll see the results next.

Lengthscale Effect on GP Smoothness

Lengthscale controls GP smoothness Small $l$ = wiggly • Large $l$ = smooth
Key Insight Lengthscales determine how the GP interpolates — shorter = more local detail, longer = smoother trends.
Physical Insights · 01

Partial Dependence Analysis (PDP)

Isolating the effect of density on Mean Crossing Time

09 / 17
What is Partial Dependence?
PDP isolates the effect of a single input variable (e.g., Density) while averaging out the remaining five parameters. This reveals the true non-linear relationship between a parameter and the output.
$$\text{PDP}(x_1) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mu(x_1, \mathbf{x}_{i,-1})$$
Physical Interpretation
Low Density ($\rho < 0.4$): Crossing time increases linearly — pedestrians move freely.
Critical Transition ($\rho > 0.75$): Crossing time surges exponentially — gridlock zone where pedestrians block each other.
Scientific Conclusion
The GP surrogate captures the smooth, continuous physical behavior with confidence intervals — revealing the exponential gridlock transition that raw noisy samples would miss.

Active Learning Chapter

Why Active Learning? Closed-Loop Algorithmic Engine

Iterative Bayesian Optimization Workflow for Autonomous Simulation Control

10 / 17
Methodology
Instead of static offline sampling, Active Learning constructs an autonomous feedback loop between surrogate predictions and simulation execution:
  1. Initialize: Train GP on $N=256$ Sobol QMC seeds.
  2. Predict: Evaluate continuous acquisition metric $\alpha(x)$ over 6D hypercube.
  3. Select: Identify point $x^* = \arg\max \alpha(x)$ of peak uncertainty.
  4. Simulate: Execute 10-seed CAFM Monte Carlo ensemble at $x^*$.
  5. Update: Augment dataset $\mathcal{D} \leftarrow \mathcal{D} \cup (x^*, y^*)$ and retrain GP.
Autonomous Closed-Loop Active Learning Iterative Model Self-Refinement
Key Insight Active learning continuously minimizes global surrogate error using minimal expensive simulator evaluations.

Benchmark Metrics

Universal Evaluation Framework & Composite Metric

4-Axis Weighted Scoring Combining Accuracy, Efficiency, Exploration & Runtime

12 / 17
Composite Evaluation Score Formula
To ensure unbiased comparison, strategies are ranked via the Notebook Implementation Composite Metric:
$$\text{Composite Score} = 0.50 \cdot S_{\text{Pred}} + 0.20 \cdot S_{\text{Eff}} + 0.15 \cdot S_{\text{Expl}} + 0.15 \cdot S_{\text{Runtime}}$$
Four Evaluation Axes
• 1. Prediction (50%): Surrogate accuracy ($R^2$ & RMSE).
• 2. Efficiency (20%): Variance reduction per simulation (($\Delta R^2$ Gain X 1000)/sim. min).
• 3. Exploration (15%): Boundary % vs Interior hypercube coverage.
• 4. Runtime (15%): Candidate search computational cost.
Derived Metrics
We also track R² Gain, Advantage over Baseline, Simulation Cost, Speedup, and Diversity to provide a holistic view of each method's performance.

Key Insight Evaluating strategies across all 4 axes prevents selecting computationally slow or boundary-biased acquisition functions.
